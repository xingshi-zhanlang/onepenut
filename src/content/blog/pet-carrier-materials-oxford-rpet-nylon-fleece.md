Material is the quietest decision in product development and usually the one that decides everything else. It sets the price point, the durability, the washability, the hand feel your customer notices in the first three seconds, and — increasingly — whether your product can be sold into markets that ask questions about sustainability.

Here is what the common specifications actually mean, without the marketing layer.

## Oxford polyester and denier ratings

Most soft pet carriers are made from Oxford weave polyester. "Oxford" describes the weave structure, not the fibre — it produces a slightly basket-like texture that resists tearing and holds its shape well, which is exactly what a bag that gets dragged, folded and stuffed needs.

The number you see in front of the "D" is denier, which measures the thickness of the yarn. It is a weight measurement, not a quality rating, and it is widely misunderstood.

- **210D–300D** — lighter, softer, more packable. Good for pouches, lightweight carriers and products where folded size matters more than abrasion resistance.
- **600D** — the workhorse for travel carriers. A reasonable balance of durability, structure and weight.
- **900D–1680D** — heavier and stiffer, used for luggage-grade gear where abrasion resistance is the priority.

Two products can both say "600D Oxford" and behave completely differently, because denier says nothing about coating, backing, yarn quality or construction. A coated 300D can outperform an uncoated 600D for water resistance; a well-made 600D can feel cheaper than a carefully finished 420D.

So when you compare suppliers: ask about denier, but do not stop there. Ask what else is on the fabric.

## What coatings and finishes do

- **PU (polyurethane) backing** — the most common. Adds water resistance and body. Look for water-based PU if you want a lower-impact option; it performs comparably and is increasingly available.
- **PVC backing** — heavier duty, fully waterproof, but carries a recycling problem and is restricted in some markets. Worth avoiding unless the product genuinely requires it.
- **Water-repellent finish (DWR)** — keeps light rain out and lets the fabric breathe better than a full coating. Good for travel products that live in a car.
- **Anti-bacterial or odour treatment** — popular for carriers and liners. Ask what the treatment is based on and whether documentation is available.

## RPET recycled fabric

RPET is polyester made from recycled plastic — usually post-consumer bottles — rather than virgin petroleum feedstock. Functionally, a well-produced RPET behaves very close to virgin polyester: same handle, same durability, same printability.

The practical differences are:

- **Cost** — usually a modest premium, sometimes none at higher volumes.
- **Documentation** — this is the real differentiator. A credible RPET supplier can provide traceability paperwork and, where relevant, certification. Without that paper, "recycled" is a claim rather than a specification.
- **Colour matching** — recycled feedstock can make certain bright or highly specific colours harder to hit consistently. If exact colour is critical, confirm this early.

For brands selling into Europe especially, RPET is often the simplest credible sustainability story available, because it is verifiable rather than aspirational. But the verification is the product. If a supplier cannot show you the paperwork, you do not have a recycled product — you have a description of one.

## Nylon

Nylon costs more than polyester and earns it in specific situations:

- **Abrasion resistance** — better than polyester, which matters for leashes, harness webbing and anything that drags on concrete.
- **Softness** — a distinctly smoother, less plastic hand feel at the same weight. On a harness or a collar that sits against skin and fur, this is noticeable.
- **Strength to weight** — you can use a lighter nylon and get the same strength, which is why good harnesses often feel unexpectedly light.

For travel carriers, polyester is usually the better commercial choice. For walking gear that has to survive daily use and constant friction, nylon is often worth the premium.

## Sherpa, fleece and plush linings

The lining is the part the animal actually experiences, and it is the part that most affects whether a nervous pet settles.

- **Sherpa fleece** — the traditional answer. Deep, warm and cosy; excellent for cooler climates and for anxious animals that want to burrow.
- **Short-pile plush** — easier to brush clean and less prone to matting than sherpa, with a similar comfort effect. Often the better choice for a product that will be used frequently.
- **Quilted cotton** — breathable, cooler and easy to wash. Better fitted to warm markets and summer ranges.

Whatever the lining, insist on these three things: it should be **removable**, **machine washable**, and **attached with something that stays attached**. A lining that slides out of position mid-journey is worse than no lining at all.

## Mesh, webbing and the parts nobody asks about

Mesh is structural, not decorative. Look for a mesh that is fine enough to contain claws but open enough to move air — and check that it is stitched into a seam, not simply laid on top of the panel.

Webbing is where cheap products fail first. Polypropylene webbing is inexpensive but degrades with UV exposure. Nylon webbing is stronger and more UV-stable. Width matters too: a 25 mm strap supporting a 7 kg animal behaves differently from a 15 mm one.

Hardware should be matched to the load. Metal D-rings and welded rings for anything load-bearing, acetal side-release buckles for closures that take repeated openings. The buckle is the part most likely to be squeezed in a hurry, so it should be the part you do not economise on.

## How to specify material for your price point

A rough decision order that works for most projects:

1. **Fix the retail price first**, then work backwards. Material follows price, not the other way around.
2. **Choose denier by use case**, not by the biggest number available.
3. **Decide on the lining early.** It changes the pattern, the cost and often the animal's acceptance.
4. **Treat recycled content as a documentation project**, not a fabric choice. If you need the claim, budget the time to verify it.
5. **Sample in the actual fabric you intend to sell.** Approving a sample in a nicer fabric than production is one of the most common and most expensive mistakes in soft goods.
