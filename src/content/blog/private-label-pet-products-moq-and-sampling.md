Most first-time buyers approach private label sourcing with two questions: how many do I have to order, and how long will it take. Both are reasonable questions, and both are usually answered badly — either with a single confident number that turns out not to apply, or with "it depends", which is true but useless.

What follows is how the process actually runs, so you can plan around it instead of being surprised by it.

## What MOQ actually covers

Minimum order quantity is not one number. It is the sum of several separate constraints, and whichever is largest is the one you will be quoted.

**Fabric minimums.** Mills sell fabric by the roll. If a colour or specification is not a stock item, you may be buying a full roll whether you need it or not. This is why your MOQ can differ dramatically between colours of the same product — and why choosing an existing stock colour can be the cheapest decision in the whole project.

**Hardware minimums.** Custom buckles, printed tags, embossed logos and bespoke zipper pulls are often tooled items. Tooling has its own setup cost, and the supplier will spread that cost across the order — which means small orders carry a disproportionate penalty.

**Production minimums.** Cutting, sewing and assembly lines have a setup time that does not shrink for small runs. Below a certain quantity the line is simply not efficient, and the price per unit climbs steeply.

**Packaging minimums.** Custom printed boxes, headers and hangtags have their own minimums, sometimes higher than the product itself. A buyer can be surprised to find that the packaging is the part forcing the quantity up.

Because these constraints stack, the honest answer to "what is your MOQ" is always "for which model, which colour and which packaging?" A supplier who gives you a single number without asking those questions is either not listening or not planning to customise anything for you.

## The sampling loop

This is the part that most timelines get wrong, because people treat sampling as a single step. In practice it is a loop, and you should budget for it to run more than once.

**1. Brief.** You send what you have — a sketch, a reference sample, a competitor product, photos, or just a written description with a target price and market. The more precise the brief, the fewer loops follow.

**2. First sample.** The factory produces a physical sample. This is where you learn which of your assumptions were wrong. Fabric drape, hardware weight, stitching density and colour rarely match expectations on the first pass.

**3. Correction round.** You give consolidated feedback. The single most useful thing you can do here is to mark the sample physically — tape, tags, a marked-up photo — rather than describing the change in prose. Ambiguity in this step is the main cause of extra rounds.

**4. Approval sample.** Once corrections are made, you approve a sample that becomes the reference standard. That approved sample is what the production team measures against, and it is what a third-party inspector will compare the finished goods to.

Two things follow from this. First, keep your approved sample, and keep it in good condition — it is your legal and technical reference. Second, if a supplier is vague about whether sampling costs anything, clarity on that point is worth more than a small saving.

## Where lead time actually goes

Lead time is usually quoted as one number, but it is made of four distinct phases with very different behaviour.

- **Sampling** — driven by how many correction rounds you need. Your own responsiveness is often the biggest variable here.
- **Material procurement** — near zero for stock materials, and a long pole for custom fabrics, dyed-to-match colours or bespoke hardware.
- **Production** — fairly predictable once the line is running, and this is the number suppliers quote most confidently.
- **Transit** — varies enormously between sea, rail, air and express, and is the phase most affected by things outside anyone's control.

When a supplier quotes you a lead time, ask which of these four it includes. A quote that covers production only and excludes transit and material procurement is not wrong, but it is often not what the buyer heard.

## What raises your order quantity

If you need to bring a quantity down, these are the levers, roughly in order of effectiveness:

1. **Use an existing product and customise branding only.** No new tooling, no new fabric, stock packaging. This is by far the cheapest route to a private label product.
2. **Choose stock colours and materials.** Eliminates fabric and hardware minimums.
3. **Skip custom packaging on the first order.** Use stock boxes with a custom label, then upgrade once the product has proven itself.
4. **Accept a higher unit price.** Smaller runs are possible; they simply cost more per unit. Whether that trade is worth it depends on whether you are testing a market or scaling one.

For a first order, most sensible buyers do all four. The goal of a trial order is not margin — it is information.

## What to send a supplier

A brief that gets answered quickly contains:

- What the product is and who it is for
- Your target market and the price you need it to retail at
- Your target landed cost, even if it is a guess
- Reference images or a physical sample
- Which elements must be custom and which can be standard
- Your timeline, including whether it is real or aspirational

The last point matters more than people admit. A supplier who knows your timeline is genuine will sequence the work differently than one who assumes it is a wish.

## A note on numbers
