Buying a pet carrier is one of those decisions that looks simple until the bag arrives and the animal refuses to go in it — or the airline gate staff measure it and say no. Size is the single biggest reason carriers get returned, and it is also the easiest thing to get right if you work in the correct order.

The correct order is: start with the journey, then the animal, then the bag. Most people do it backwards.

## Start with the journey, not the bag

A carrier has to satisfy a set of hard constraints before comfort even enters the conversation.

**In-cabin air travel.** Every airline publishes its own maximum under-seat dimensions, and they are not standardised. A bag that fits one carrier's cabin may be refused on another. The most common figure quoted is around 17 × 11 × 8 inches (roughly 43 × 28 × 20 cm), but treat that as a starting point rather than a rule. Check the specific airline and route you are flying, because the same airline can use different aircraft with different under-seat clearances.

There is also a soft constraint that catches people out: the bag must be able to compress. A carrier that is exactly at the limit with a rigid frame will not slide under a seat that has a curved support bar.

**Checked or cargo travel.** Here the constraint flips — you want the opposite of compressibility. A rigid, well-ventilated crate with secure, ideally metal, fastenings is the right answer, and it needs to be large enough for the animal to stand, turn around and lie down naturally.

**Car and everyday use.** Constraints are looser, so comfort and convenience dominate. A carrier that is easy to open, easy to clean and stable when placed on a seat will get used far more often than one that is technically superior but awkward.

## Measuring the animal properly

Two measurements matter, and a third is worth knowing.

**Length** is measured from the base of the tail to the tip of the nose, with the pet standing. This is the measurement that decides the interior length, and you want the pet to be able to lie down fully stretched out — not curled.

**Height** is measured from the floor to the top of the head, or to the top of the ears if the ears stand up. Add a little clearance so the pet can stand without its head pressing against the roof, but not so much that the animal slides around in transit.

**Weight** matters because it drives the structure, not just the fabric. A carrier rated for a 5 kg cat is built differently from one rated for a 12 kg dog, even if the outer dimensions are similar. A soft bag with a light base board will sag under a heavier animal, and a sagging carrier is an uncomfortable one.

If your pet sits between two sizes, go up — but verify the larger size still fits your actual travel constraint. This is where the journey-first rule pays off.

## Reading a size chart honestly

Size charts are usually written by the manufacturer and describe the *external* dimensions, because that is what the airline measures. Interior space is what the animal experiences. The gap between the two is the wall thickness, the padding and the base board, and it can easily be 3–5 cm on each side.

When you compare carriers, compare like with like:

- Are the listed dimensions external or internal?
- Is the weight limit a tested structure rating or an estimate?
- Does the size refer to the bag laid flat, or as it sits when carried?

A supplier who can answer those three questions clearly is a supplier whose chart you can rely on. If a listing only gives one number, ask.

## The mistakes that cause most returns

**Sizing for a kitten or puppy, not the adult.** Kittens in particular grow fast. A carrier bought at eight weeks may be visibly too small by six months.

**Assuming the animal will accept it immediately.** Introduce the carrier at home, open, with bedding and treats, for a few days before the first journey. A carrier that smells like home is accepted far more easily.

**Ignoring ventilation.** Mesh panels on at least two sides keep air moving. On a warm day, a carrier with one small mesh window will get uncomfortable quickly.

**Buying without checking the opening.** A top-loading carrier is much easier to place a nervous cat into than a front-loading one. If your pet dislikes being handled, this matters more than an extra centimetre of interior space.

**Forgetting the base.** A removable, washable, rigid base board is the difference between a carrier that still looks good after a year and one that does not.
